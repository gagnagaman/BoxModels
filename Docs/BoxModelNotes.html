A review of a hierarchy of box models

This review deals with box models of varying sophistication. After general introduction one section deals with a simplified version of Stommels original 2 box model. The next section deals with a slightly more complex model, i.e. Rahmstorfs 4 box model. These two models are purely thermohaline. However, this discussion is followed by a section on the Gnanadesikan model, which also includes the effects of equatorial upwelling, near surface Ekman transport and eddy transport in the southern ocean. In each case the model steady state behaviour is examined, and stability analyses performed.

Introduction to box models

Box models were originally deived from an analogy with capillary flow (Stommel 1967). Stommel imagined two well mixed boxes connected by a hose with a volume flow q that advects fluid tracers such as temperature and salinity. According to this formulation, the only valid differencing scheme is upstream differencing.

Consider a box of volume V occupied by a fluid of density ρ with . Through the side there is an inflow (q > 0) of water with a temperature that differs by ΔT from the water in the box. Likewise the salinity of the inflowing water differs by ΔS from the water in the box. At the surface there are fluxes of salinity (𝒫) and heat (ℋ), and to conserve volume we have an outflow of q at the other side. Conservation of heat for this system yields ρ C V T . = ℋ + ρ C q ΔT , where the dot refers to a time derivative.The first term and ℋ have the units of "energy/time" (e.g. Watt units), and since q is a volume flux (e . g . Sv or m^3/s) the dimensions of ρ C q ΔT are also in "energy/time". Dividing through with ρ C gives V T . = H + q ΔT , where H ≡ ℋ/ρ C . In this case terms have dimensions of "volume flux × temperature" (e.g. units of m^3 K/s or Sv K) . Similarily one can write the conservation of salinity as ρ V S . = 𝒫 + ρ q ΔS , where all terms have the dimensions of "mass/time" (e.g. units of psu × kg/s). Dividing through with ρ puts this in dimensions of "volume flux × salinity" and changes the conservation equation to V S . = P + q ΔS , where P ≡ 𝒫/ρ . Box models are usually presented in the dimensional form of "volume flux × tracer". This form has the benifit of being compact and the equations of heat and salinity conservation look very similar. Sometimes the volumes of all boxes being treated are equal, and thus V can be cancelled (see for instance Stommels original paper, and papers by Marotzke (e.g. Marotzke 1990)). In this case the dimension of each term simply becomes "tracer/time". The flow rule most often used in box models is simply q = k Δρ. The constant k is usually prescribed ad hoc, or determined empirically. If the box model has the dimensions of "volume flux × tracer" k must have the dimensions of (volume)^2/(mass × time) , if the model dimensions are "tracer/time" then k should have the dimensions of volume/(mass × time). In the latter case it can be convenient to use κ = kρ_o instead of k. This is convenient because the linearized equation of state Δρ = ρ_0 ( β ΔS - αΔT) already has the ρ_0 factor, so kΔρ = κ(β ΔS - αΔT). The bracket is dimensionless and κ will have dimensions of 1/time. Another way of thinking about box models is to view them as derived from fundamental primitive equations, with upstream differencing applied on an extremely coarse grid (see Tzipperman etal. 1996, Cessi and Young 1994). In this view it is only natural to ask if other differencing schemes could be used. (e.g., with more boxes one could presumably use centered differencing). This raises questions about the justifiation and appropriateness of each difference scheme, and how a choice of a scheme affects the qualitiave features of the system. That line of inquiry is potentially quite open-ended, and is not pursued in here. We will simply adhere to the conceptual tub-and-hose view of box models described above

The Stommel model

The box model devised by Stommel (1967) is very simple. It consists of two well mixed boxes that exchange temperature and salinity according to the flow rule q = k Δρ , where Δρ is the density difference between the boxes. If we focus on the system obtained when q > 0 we obtain { V1 T1 . = H1 + q (T2 - T1), V2 T1 . = H1 + q (T1 - T2) , V1 S1 . = P1 + q (S2 - S1), V2 S2 . = P2 + q (S1 - S2) } In Stommels original model q is allowed to have either sign. In a two box model it is easy to use upstream differencing in both cases ( q > 0 and q < 0 ), - one simply needs to replace q by its absaloute value. In more complicated models (3 boxes or more) a separate set of equations is needed for the upstream differencing of the q < 0 case. However, it is for most purposes sufficient to restrict the system to q > 0 . What is learned in that case is easily carried over to the other case, where q is allowed to have either sign.

Conservation of salinity in the system results in the first eigenvalue being zero. The numerator of the second eigenvalue can be written as 2 ( γ F - q^2 ) . If we restrict (F,q) to the the equilibrium curve (i.e., γ F = - q^2 - σ q) we get ( γ F - q^2 ) = (-2 q^2 - σ q) . This is positive if q > -σ/2 = q_c but negative if q < -σ/2 = q_c . This means that second eigenvalue will be negative on the upper branch of the equilibrium curve, but it will be positive on the lower branch of the equilibrium curve. But positive eigenvalues indicate that the system is linearly unstable. Thus we reach the following conclusion: The upper branch of the equilibrium curve is stable, and the lower branch is unstable. The instability is usually referred to as advectice instability, and the mecanism behind it has been examined in great detail by several authors (see for instance Marotske (1989) or Tzipperman et.al. (1994) ). To gain better appreciation of the mechanism, it is convenient to define FormBox[ RowBox[{" ", RowBox[{"ΔS", " ", "≡", " ", RowBox[{"(", RowBox[{ RowBox[{"S2", " ", "-", " ", FormBox[S1), ""]}], ","}], " "}]}]}], ] which is negative for F>0; assume equal volumes, and write the salinity equations as (ΔS)• = Π - 2 q ΔS , where Π=P2 - P1. Linearizing this (ΔS → ΔS +δS, q → q + δq) gives (ΔS • + δS )• = Π - 2 q ΔS - 2 δq ΔS - 2 q δS, wich results in the following evolution equation for the salinity anomaly: δS • = - 2 δq ΔS - 2 q δS . This shows that the evolution of salinity anomaly (δS) is (to first order) composed of the advection of the mean salinity difference by the flow anomaly (δq ΔS) and the advection of the salinity anomaly by the mean flow (q δS). From the flow rule we get δq = kβδS, wich shows that the flow anomaly is of the same sign as the salinity anomaly. This allows us to write the evolution equation as δS • = - 2 δq ΔS - 2 q δS = (- 2 k β ΔS - 2 q ) δS However, we already have ΔS= -F S_o/q and thus (- 2 k β ΔS - 2 q) = (+ 2 k β F S_0/q - 2 q) = 2 ( γF/q - q) , and the evolution equation becomes δS • = 2 ( γF/q - q) δS If the term 2 ( γF/q - q) is positive the salinity anomaly will grow (the system is unstable) if it is negative the salinity anomaly will decay (the system is stable). However, the sign of 2 ( γF/q - q) is the same as that of γ F - q^2 (the numerator of the second eigenvalue) , and we had already established that on the upper branch of the equilibrium curve this quantity is negative and on the lower branch it is positive. The destabilizing term (the one that that contributes to a postive sign of 2 ( γF/q - q) ) is γF/q = - δq ΔS , the advection of the mean salinity difference by the flow anomaly, and the stabilizing term (the one that that contributes to a negative sign) is q δS , the advection of the salinity anomaly by the mean flow. Thus we are led to the following picture. On the upper branch the advection of the perturbation salinity by the mean flow is larger than the advection of the mean salinity difference by the flow anomaly. This means that any disturbance is eventually damped out and the system is stable. However when q is reduced to the critical point ( q_c) these two processes become equal in magnitude, and if the q decreases further, the destabilizing advection of the mean salinity difference by the perturbation velocity dominates. Hence the name "advective instability".

Analyses of the Rahmstorf model version I

First we must define the flow rule with Δρ = ( ρ_ 2 - ρ_1 )

Box 1 represents the Southern Ocean, and box 2 the Northern high latitudes (where deep water forms) and the deep ocean. Box 3 occupies the upper ocean, its depth D is the pycnocline depth. The flow into the northern box is driven by a standard thermohaline flow rule q_n = k Δρ . However, in this model D is allowed to vary, and scaling arguments can be employed to show that k= η D^2 is appropriate where η≡ gC/ρ_o L_y%n ∂_y f . The various parameters included in η are the graviational acceleration g, a reference density ρ_o, L_y%n is the north south distance over which the gradient in the layer depth occurs, and ∂_y f represents the β-effect. Using reasonable values for the parameters in η (g = 10 m/s^2 , FormBox[ RowBox[{ RowBox[{ RowBox[{ RowBox[{ RowBox[{ FormBox[ L_y = 1500 km, , ""], "C"}], "=", "0.15"}], ",", " ", ρ_o = 1035 kg/m^3 and ∂_y f = 2×10^-11 m^-1 s^-1}], ")"}], " "}], ] gives η ≈ 50 m^4 s^-1 kg^-1 . The flow from the south into the equatorial box consists of an equatorward Ekman transport q_τ = τ/(ρ_0 f) L_w and a poleward transport by eddy activity. The latter is taken to depend on the thickness gradient, i.e. q_eddy = A_I L_w D/L_y%s . Here, L_w is the circumfere of the earth in Drake Passage latitudes, τ is the windstress, f is the corriolis parameter in Drake Passage latitudes, A_I is an eddy diffusivity coefficient and L_y%s is the distance used to estimate the thickness gradient in the south. If we define ε= A_I L_w /L_y%s the net transport from the south is q_s = q_τ - ε D The upwelling into the thermocline must balance the turbulent downward mixing, i.e. q_u = K_v A/D , where K_v is vertical turbulent diffusion coefficient and A is the area of the equatorial thermocline. If we define μ= K_v A we have q_u = μ/D . Following Gnanandesikan we can take 2.44 × 10^14 m^2 as the area of the equatorial thermocline, and K_v = 0.15 cm^2/s to get μ = 3.75 × 10^9 m^4/s . Similarily we take FormBox[ RowBox[{ SubscriptBox["A", StyleBox["I", FontSlant->"Italic"]], " ", "=", " ", 1000 m^2/s}], ] , L^s_y = 1500 km, and L_w = 25000 km to obtain ε=1.66 × 10^4 m^2/s . If we take τ=1.5 Pa as the windstress in the Drake Passage gap we get q_τ = 36 Sv . Since the total transport is conserved we have q_n = ηΔρ D^2 = q_s + q_u = q_τ - ε D + μ/D . This equation can be used to derive a cubic equation for D. The different limits of that equation produce results that are in broad agreement with OGCM model results, see Gnanadesikan (1999) for details. For our purposes, we use this equation to get a relationship between q_n, q_τ and D , viz. D(q_n - q_τ) + εD^2 - μ = 0. A solution of this quadratic is D= (2 ε)^-1 ((q_τ - q_n) ± @(q_n - q_τ)^2 + 4 εμ) . The positive solution is always real but the negative solution is not physical, as it is always less than zero. Thus using the positive solution we have a unique relationship between q_n and D. The equations for temperature and salinity are { V1 T1 . = H1 + q_s (T2 - T1), V2 T2 . = H2 + q_n (T3 - T2), V3 T3 . = H3 + q_s (T1 - T3) + q_u (T2 - T3) , V1 S1 . = P1 + q_s (S2 - S1), V2 S2 . = P2 + q_n (S3 - S2), V3 S3 . = P3 + q_s (S1 - S3) + q_u (S2 - S3) } Since we use upstream differencing, we are assuming that FormBox[ RowBox[{ FormBox[q_n, ""], ",", q_s, ","}], ] q_u > 0. As before the connection between the freshwaterfluxes and the salinity flux is F 1 S_0 = - P1 , F 2 S_0 = - P2 and (F1 + F2) S_0 = P3 . Before continuing we must derive expressions for Δρ. There are two possible choices, the difference between the density of the northern box and the equatorial box, or the difference between the northern box and the southern box. We choose the density difference as the difference between the northern/deep and equatorial boxes, i.e., Δρ = β (S2 - S3) - α (T2 - T3) . At steady state we have (S2 - S3) = -P1/q_n = F2 S_0/q_n and defining θ ≡ (T2 -T3) we get Δρ = β (-F2 S_0 /q_n) -αθ. This yields the following equation for q_n q_n^2 + η D^2 αθ + η D^2 β S_o F2 = 0 Notice that if we use k = ηD^2 this equation becomes q_n^2 + k αθ + k β S_o F2 = 0 which is identical to the result derived in version II of the Rahmstorf model. If we use k=15 Sv m^3/kg as was used above we get D = @k/η ≈ 550 m . With Rahmstorfs value of k=18.5 we get D ≈ 600 m , using k=2 (Marotzke) yields a pycnocline depth of 200 m and using k=200 (Keeling) yields 2000 m . We have a quadratic equation relating D and q_n , and unique solution D = D(q_n) . Thus, one way of stating the difference in the thermohaline effects between the Rahmstorf model and the Gnanadesikan model is that in the first the proportionality factor k, in the flow rule q = k Δρ , is a constant, whereas in the latter, k is a function of the pycnocline depth, and thus a function of the overturning rate. Comparison with a Rahmstorf model

The Rahmstorf model has a quadratic equilibrium curve, whereas the Gnanadesikan model has a curve that is "more triangular". The latter model has two things that the first one lacks, the southern ocean processes, and a variable pycnocline. Now we will remove the southern ocean effects (by setting q_τ = 0 Sv and ε≈ 0) but increase the vertical diffusion to 1 cm^2/s . The driving temperature and the efficiency parameter are set at -6 C and 0.05 respectively, to get a curve wich turns around close to 15 Sv. The resulting curve is then compared with the one obtained with Rahmstorfs model using θ = - 6 C and with k = 19 to make the two curves intersect the y axis at the same q value.

As previously, the Gnanadesikan model is much less sensitive to freshwater forcing than the Rahmstorf model is, however the curve is not nearly as asymmetric as in the previous comparison, the reason is (as can be seen in the previous section) that the southern processes are a big factor in generating the asymmetry. However they are not the only factor, as can be seen by the fact that the equilibrium curve for the Gnanadesikan model is still not quite symmetric, while the curve for the Rahmstorf model is. Both curves intersect the y axis at ≈ 23 Sv, but while the turnaround point for the Rahmstorf model occurs at half this value (11 - 12 Sv) the turnaround point for the Gnanadesikan model occurs at around 14 Sv which is about 60 % of the intersection value.
